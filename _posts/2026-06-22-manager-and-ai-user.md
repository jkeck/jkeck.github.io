Most of the writing about AI in engineering teams focuses on the IC experience. How to prompt well, how to review AI-generated code, how to avoid shipping garbage. That's all real and worth talking about. But there's another angle that gets less attention: what it's actually like to use AI as an engineering manager, for the management work itself.

I want to be specific here, because "AI makes me more productive" is both true and useless as a thing to say. What actually changed for me is more granular than that.

The most consistent shift has been on writing and communication, not in a ghost-writing sense, but as a thinking tool before and during the writing. Before a hard 1:1, I'll often use AI to think through the conversation. "What am I actually trying to say?" "What's the other person likely to hear?" "Where am I being fuzzy?" It's less about generating words and more about forcing clarity before I'm in the middle of the conversation. The output is that I go in less reactive. I'm not winging it, and I'm not performing a script. I've just thought it through more than I would have otherwise.

The other place it shows up regularly is technical research. As an EM who tries to stay close to the work, there's always a gap between what I know and what my team is deep in. AI has lowered the cost of closing that gap quickly, getting up to speed on an approach before a design conversation, understanding a tradeoff well enough to have an informed opinion without pretending. When I do that research and it comes up in conversation, I'm transparent about it. "I was reading about this" is just true, and colleagues generally appreciate that more than somebody who shows up acting like they knew it all along.

This extends to understanding how the team is actually performing, not just what they're working on. The ability to dig into data about team performance has always existed, but AI makes it much easier to pull on those threads and surface patterns that used to take real effort to find. It's easy to mistake throughput for success. The number of tickets closed or PRs merged tells you something is moving, but not whether what's moving is the right thing. Figuring out whether increased throughput is actually translating into user or business value is a different question, and it's one an API call can't answer for you. The data can tell you a story, but understanding which story is the right one is still on me.

Then there's the more complicated one: staying hands-on in the code. AI has genuinely lowered the barrier to doing IC work. Time that I used to spend on certain management overhead gets compressed, and the ramp-up cost on an unfamiliar part of the codebase is lower. So in theory I could be in the code more than before.

But "could" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The judgment about whether to be in the code is entirely mine, and AI doesn't help with it at all. The risk of stepping on ownership, blocking growth, or sending an implicit signal that I don't trust someone to handle something? That's the same as it's always been, maybe more acute when the team is also moving faster. AI gives you the access but it doesn't give you the wisdom about when to use it.

There's also a broader context here worth naming. The industry trend toward flatter teams, where everyone is closer to buildling product, makes staying close to the code feel less like an EM doing IC work on the side and more like a shared baseline. With AI lowering the cost of jumping in, that strength can show up differently: less about proving I can still code, more about picking up the thing that unblocks someone else, clearing space so the team can stay focused on what matters most. The judgment about when that's helpful versus when it gets in the way is, again, entirely mine.

What's interesting to me is how much of this is largely invisible. People see me using AI on technical work. The management-side usage, the conversation prep, the research, the drafting, mostly happens off to the side. I don't think that's a problem, but it does mean the role of AI in management work is underrepresented in how teams actually talk about this stuff. My team probably assumes I use it, but it's not something that really gets discussed.

Most writing about AI in engineering teams focuses on IC work because that's where the productivity signal is clearest. What I've found is that it's changed the scaffolding of my job without touching the center. The research, the prep, the data are all different. The career conversations, the coaching, the work of helping someone figure out what they want to become are exactly the same. Which is probably the right distribution.
